"We have lived enough for others; let us live at least this tail-end of life for ourselves."

"The greatest thing in the world is to know how to belong to oneself."

"Behind the horseman sits black care" — Horace, quoted by Montaigne: flee the world, and if you don't first master the passions you carry, you only take yourself along: *"He took himself along with him."* Reserve a room "entirely free" behind the shop — "talking and laughing as though we had no wife, no children, no possessions… so that when the time comes to lose them it will be nothing new for us to do without them."

"In lonely places, be a crowd unto yourself." — Tibullus

---

**Connection to [[How Distant Everything Is!]]:**

Cioran treats solitude as the final, inescapable human condition — the deepest death is death in solitude, and only there do tears truly burn. Montaigne, writing four centuries earlier, meets him at the same crossroads from the opposite direction: for him solitude isn't the terror of being cut off, but an art — the room behind the shop where one rehearses, calmly and in advance, the losses Cioran says we flee with deathbed audiences. Both agree that retreating "to a faraway corner" is worthless if you drag your theatrics along; the difference is that Cioran finds in that locked room an abyss, while Montaigne finds a self worth belonging to.
