> A mother gave her children Aesop's fables to read, in the hope of educating and improving their minds; but they very soon brought the book back, and the eldest, wise beyond his years, delivered himself as follows: This is no book for us; it's much too childish and stupid. You can't make us believe that foxes and wolves and ravens are able to talk; we've got beyond stories of that kind! In these young hopefuls you have the enlightened Rationalists of the future.

— Arthur Schopenhauer, *Studies in Pessimism*, "A Few Parables" (trans. T. Bailey Saunders)

**Connection to [[Life is a senseless evil]]:**

Tolstoy asks, half in mockery, whether only he and Schopenhauer are wise enough to see through life — and Schopenhauer answers with a parable about children who think they have outgrown fables. The joke cuts the same way in both notes: the claim to have seen through everything is itself the least examined position. The children who dismiss Aesop as "too childish and stupid" are the same figure as the man who dismisses life as a "senseless evil" — each mistakes the loss of a story for the arrival of wisdom. Which is why the source note's own topic is a *humorous* perspective on life: the funniest claim is always the one made by someone who thinks he is beyond being fooled.
