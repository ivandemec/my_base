> I spoke by almost keeping silent. I’m a master at doing that, I’ve done it all my life and I’ve lived through entire tragedies on my own without saying a word … I kept on being silent; especially when she was around, right up until yesterday – but why? Because I’m a proud man …

> The silence continued, and I suddenly felt next to my hair, at my temple, the cold touch of iron – the revolver. You may ask: did I really hope to survive? I will answer you, and this is God’s own truth: I had no such hope.

— Fyodor Dostoyevsky, *The Gentle Spirit*

*Source: [Fyodor Dostoyevsky: The Gentle Spirit — The Sphere of Ideas](https://thesphereofideas.wordpress.com/fyodor-dostoyevsky-the-gentle-spirit-2/)*

**Connection to [[I don’t want harmony]]:**

Both passages are Dostoyevsky refusing a settlement on behalf of wounded human feeling. In *The Brothers Karamazov* the refusal is loud and rhetorical — Ivan hands back his entrance ticket rather than accept a harmony bought at the price of a child's tear. In *The Gentle Spirit* the same refusal has gone mute and inward: "I spoke by almost keeping silent. I'm a master at doing that." The proud man who "lived through entire tragedies … without saying a word" is Ivan's rebellion after the argument dies out — the ticket returned not in an eloquent speech but in a silence sustained right up to the revolver at his temple. Where Ivan still addresses Alyosha, still argues the case before God, the narrator of *The Gentle Spirit* has stopped addressing anyone at all. It is the same refusal of a cheap reconciliation, carried past speech into the act.
