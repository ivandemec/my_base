# A Relational Interpretation of Temporality in Physics and Metaphysics

![[Pasted image 20260822084156.png]]
The following essay—concerned with the reality, illusiveness, or even non‑existence of time—is admittedly technical, and its central argument reaches beyond the usual scope of this page. Still, because Spinoza has decisive things to say about eternity and infinity, and because the essay engages his views directly, I thought it worthwhile to share it here. So, here it is.

# Abstract

Time is not a fundamental background parameter but the emergent order produced by changing relations. Building on quantum gravity approaches that remove time from fundamental equations, Barbour’s notion of “timeless instants,” and Rovelli’s relational quantum mechanics, this paper develops a relational ontology in which entities exist only through nested layers of relations—Russian dolls all the way down.

Within this framework, temporality arises whenever these relational layers shift, echoing Aristotle’s insight that time is the ordered unfolding of change — the way change arranges itself into ‘before’ and ‘after.’

General relativity reinforces this view by showing that temporal flow varies with spatial and gravitational relations. Spinoza’s distinction between duration and eternity provides the metaphysical counterpart: duration expresses the temporal aspect of finite relational modes, while eternity expresses the non temporal essence of substance.

Together, these perspectives converge on a unified thesis: time is the immediate simultaneous structural ordering of relational change, not an absolute container.

This relational conception integrates modern physics and classical metaphysics into a coherent, non substantival account of temporality.

Introduction: The Problem of Time

My reflections on the nature of time were initially prompted by scientific discussions suggesting that time may be illusory or may not exist at the most fundamental level of physical description. Certain approaches in quantum gravity eliminate time from the basic equations (Kiefer 2012; Isham 1993). Julian Barbour argues that the universe consists of “timeless instants” (Barbour 1999), and Carlo Rovelli emphasizes that the fundamental laws of physics do not contain time as a universal parameter (Rovelli 2018).

These claims raise a profound question: if time is not fundamental, how should we understand its apparent ubiquity in experience and its indispensable role in scientific explanation?

This question led me to a deeper insight: nothing exists in complete isolation. Every entity—down to the smallest physical system—ontologically exists only through its relations with other entities. Once this relational structure of reality became clear to me, the insight followed immediately: if all things exist only through their relations, then time itself must arise simultaneously from the changes within those relations.

Time is not an independent background against which events unfold; it is the structural order generated by relational variation.

This relational conception of time resonates strongly with two major intellectual traditions: Rovelli’s relational interpretation of quantum mechanics and Spinoza’s metaphysics of duration and eternity. Both frameworks reject the notion of time as an absolute container and instead treat temporality as a feature arising directly from relational structure.

Relational Ontology

To say that nothing exists without relating is to deny that objects possess self contained identities. An entity is what it is only through the network of interactions, dependencies, and causal connections in which it participates. Spinoza expresses this through his doctrine of modes: finite things exist only through the causal nexus of Nature, and their persistence is inseparable from the relations that constitute them (Spinoza Ethics IIP11–IIP13).

Modern physics offers a striking parallel. In relational quantum mechanics, Rovelli argues that physical systems do not possess absolute states. A system’s properties—such as position or momentum—exist only relative to another system with which it interacts (Rovelli 1996). There is no observer independent description of a system; reality is fundamentally relational.

This convergence between metaphysics and physics supports my initial insight: relations are ontologically primary. The relational framework thus provides a bridge between metaphysical ontology and physical description, allowing us to distinguish ontological emergence—the coming into being of time as a structural feature of reality—from epistemic emergence, the way time appears in our descriptions and measurements.

Russian Doll Relationality

In this relational ontology, an entity is never a self-standing unit but a nested structure of relations—Russian dolls all the way down. What appears as a “thing” is in fact a dynamic equilibrium of determinations: internal relations forming its present drive to be, external relations shaping its encounters, and historical relations sedimented as memory, habit, and structural disposition. Each layer is itself composed of further layers, and none possesses an isolated core or independent essence.

A mode exists only through the relations that constitute it, and those relations exist only through further relations, expressing the one substance in progressively finite configurations. Time, in this framework, is not a container in which entities persist, but the ongoing transformation of these relational layers—the continual re-composition of the doll.

Duration is simply the way a relational structure maintains and modifies its coherence across encounters.

Eternity, in Spinoza’s sense, is the fact that the entire nested architecture expresses the same infinite substance, regardless of how its finite relations unfold.

Thus, to understand an entity is to understand the relational depth that makes it what it is; and to understand time is to understand the ceaseless re articulation of those relations.

Time as the Emergence of Relational Change

If relations are fundamental, then time must arise from the changes within those relations. A static relation does not generate temporality; only relational variation does.

Aristotle I: Nature as Motion

Aristotle already recognized that nature itself is inseparable from change. In Physics III.1 he writes:

“Nature has been defined as a ‘principle of motion and change,’ and it is the subject of our inquiry. We must therefore see that we understand the meaning of ‘motion’; for if it were unknown, the meaning of ‘nature’ too would be unknown.”

This insight makes motion the primary ontological fact, and thus prepares the conceptual ground for understanding time as the ordering of change.

Aristotle II: Time as the Number of Change

This leads directly to his later definition in Physics IV.11, where time is “the number of change with respect to before and after.“

Taken together, these claims express a single idea: time is not an independent entity but the ordering of change itself. In the relational framework developed here, this means that wherever relations shift, time appears; wherever relations remain fixed, temporality does not arise. Aristotle’s account thus anticipates the modern relational thesis that time is inseparable from relational variation rather than an external background in which change occurs.

Rovelli’s work makes this point explicit. In general relativity, each physical system possesses its own “proper time,” determined by its motion and by the gravitational field it experiences (Misner, Thorne & Wheeler 1973). Consequently:

• clocks on Earth run at one rate,

• clocks on GPS satellites run at another (Ashby 2003),

• clocks on rapidly moving spacecraft run at yet another.

These differences are negligible at everyday scales but become measurable when relational conditions diverge sufficiently from those on Earth’s surface. Time, therefore, is not universal; it is local and relational—and phenomenologically, it appears as the differing rhythms of change experienced from distinct relational positions.

This relational direct emergence of time contrasts with Kant’s transcendental idealism, which treats time as a form of intuition structuring all experience. In the relational view, time is not imposed by the mind but arises from the structure of reality itself—a position closer to Bergson’s durée, the lived continuity of change, yet grounded in physical interaction rather than psychological flow.

Rovelli’s Hall Example: Spatial Relations and Temporal Differences

Rovelli illustrates the relational nature of time with a simple example (Rovelli 2018). Consider a large lecture hall. I sit near the front while another person sits near the back. We are separated by only a few dozen meters, sharing the same environment and engaged in the same activity. Yet, according to general relativity, time flows at slightly different rates for the two of us.

The reason is gravitational. The Earth’s gravitational field is not perfectly uniform, and even a small difference in distance (say a dozen meters) and/or height—one or two meters—corresponds to a slight difference in gravitational potential. Einstein’s theory predicts that time runs marginally faster at higher altitudes (Einstein 1916; Pound & Rebka 1960).

Thus:

• the person sitting in the back, farther away and/or slightly higher, slightly farther from the Earth’s center, experiences time flowing imperceptibly faster;

• the person sitting in the front, closer by and/or slightly closer to the Earth’s center, experiences time flowing imperceptibly slower.

The difference is extraordinarily small—far less than a trillionth of a second during the lecture—but it is real.

The essential point is relational.

The temporal difference arises not from any intrinsic property of the individuals but from their differing spatial relations to the Earth’s gravitational field. A difference in spatial relation—height, distance, position—produces a difference in temporal relation.

This everyday example prepares us for more dramatic cases:

• clocks on GPS satellites run faster than clocks on Earth,

• clocks on fast moving spacecraft run slower,

• clocks near massive objects run significantly slower.

In all cases, the principle is the same: time is woven into the relational fabric of the world. Alter the spatial relation, and the temporal flow changes accordingly.

Spinoza: Duration and Eternity

My insight also aligns with Spinoza’s distinction between duration and eternity. Duration is the mode of existence of finite things insofar as they persist and undergo change. It corresponds to the temporal aspect of relational existence. Eternity, by contrast, is not infinite time but the non temporal existence of substance understood through its essence (Spinoza Ethics ID8).

Spinoza insists that duration and eternity are not two separate realms but two ways of understanding the same reality:

• When I consider things through their changing relations, I apprehend them under the aspect of duration.

• When I grasp things through their necessary essence, I apprehend them sub specie aeternitatis.

This dual perspective mirrors Rovelli’s distinction between the relational emergence of time and the timeless structure of fundamental physical laws. It also anticipates Lee Smolin’s argument that time is real and creative, not derivative—a claim that supports the relational thesis by emphasizing that the universe’s structure evolves through interaction (Smolin 2013).

Convergence of Physics and Metaphysics

My insight—that time exists wherever relations change—finds support in both Rovelli’s physics and Spinoza’s metaphysics.

• Rovelli demonstrates that time is local, relational, and emergent.

• Spinoza shows that duration is the temporal expression of finite relational existence.

• Bergson and Smolin affirm the creative continuity of change.

• All reject the notion of time as an absolute background.

• All treat temporality as arising from relational structure.

This relational conception also resonates with Leibniz’s classical relationalism. Leibniz argued that space is the order of coexistences and time the order of successions, denying that either is an independent substance. Although his metaphysics differs from both Spinoza’s and modern physics, the core intuition is shared: temporality is not a container but the structural ordering of relational change. The present account extends this Leibnizian insight by grounding succession not in monadic perception but in the nested, Russian doll constitution of entities and their interactions.

To deny this relational conception of time would require demonstrating the existence of entities that never enter into any relation whatsoever—an assertion unsupported by contemporary physics and incompatible with relational metaphysics.

# Conclusion

The relational conception of time developed here reveals a single, coherent insight: time is not an independent medium in which events unfold but the structural order generated by changing relations. Aristotle already saw that nature is the principle of motion and that time is the number of change; modern physics extends this insight by demonstrating that temporal flow varies with spatial and gravitational relations. Spinoza deepens the picture by distinguishing duration—the temporal expression of finite relational modes—from eternity, the non temporal essence of substance. When these perspectives are combined with a Russian doll ontology in which entities exist only through nested relational layers, a unified account emerges: wherever relations shift, time appears; wherever relations persist, duration holds; wherever essence is understood, eternity is disclosed.

Time is neither illusory nor absolute. It is the emergent rhythm of relational structure—local, specific, and inseparable from the dynamic web of interactions that constitute the world.
